# Summarize

A good summary is one you cannot summarize further. Take words out and something is lost: a fact, a decision, a reason. Every sentence carries information. Nothing describes the source instead of the content.

## Before you write

Read the whole source. Then settle two things:

- **Purpose.** Why the user wants this summary: a decision log, a catch-up for someone who missed it, a record, input for a report. The purpose decides what counts as important and how long the summary is.
- **Source.** Who wrote or said it, for whom, when. A sales call and an architecture review keep different things.

## What to keep

Keep what the reader would miss: numbers, dates, names, owners, decisions and their reasons, disagreements, consequences, open points, and changes to what was believed before.

Cut what describes the source rather than the content ("the meeting discussed", "the document covers"). Cut repetition, hedges, and pleasantries. Cut the path to a decision when only the decision matters. Keep the source's own terms for things.

Where the source is vague or contradicts itself, say so. Mark what is implied rather than stated. Add no opinion of your own.

## Order

What happened, why, what was decided, what stays open. Change the order only when the purpose asks for it; a decision log leads with the decisions.

## Length

Length follows the content and the purpose. A dense one-hour meeting can need a page. A long thread with one decision needs three lines. Take a target from the user when given. Never pick a share of the source.

## Last pass

Try to remove each sentence. If nothing is lost, remove it. Then check the other direction: every fact the reader would miss is present, once.

## Deliver

Return the summary as prose or a short list, whichever fits the purpose. Open points at the end when there are any. No introduction, no note on how the summary was made.
