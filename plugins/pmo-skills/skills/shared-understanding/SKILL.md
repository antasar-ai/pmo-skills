# Shared understanding

Pick the format that lets this reader hold this idea. One idea, one to three formats. Name the choice in one line, then deliver the idea in that format.

## Before you start

Who needs to understand it, what they already know, and the one thing they must take away. Ask when the reader or the takeaway is unknown.

## Formats

- **Decision memo**, for a reader who must choose. See below.
- **Before/after table**, for a change: one row per thing the reader will notice.
- **Reader's-eye walkthrough**, for a process change: what you will see, what you will do, what happens next.
- **Trade-off table**, for a choice between options: options against the two or three criteria that decide it, not a feature list.
- **Analogy**, for a new domain: a system the reader knows, mapped over, with the point where it breaks.
- **Stepwise reveal**, when the idea needs a build-up: one layer per step, each step complete on its own.
- **Term table**, when the words are the blocker: term, meaning, what it is not.
- **Strip the layers**, when abstraction is the blocker: remove one layer at a time until a plain sentence is left.
- **Perspectives**, when the disagreement is about viewpoint: the same topic as two or three roles see it.
- **Quiz**, to check understanding afterwards: three questions, then what each wrong answer would reveal.
- **Handover**, for a reader with no outside knowledge: everything they need, nothing they cannot check.
- **Pyramid or SCQA**, for an argument: answer first with grouped support (Minto), or situation, complication, question, answer. Other consulting structures work when they fit.
- **Structure or flow**: a tree, a diff, Mermaid, or one small HTML page or diagram. Use show-me when it is available.

When an explanation is hard to follow, revise it with the Hamburger lenses below. When a procedure must be unambiguous, write it in the STE-100 style below.

## Decision memo

The reader decides and stops reading. Build for that.

1. **The ask** in one line: what decision, by when.
2. **Situation** in two lines: what changed and why it matters now.
3. **Options**, two or three, including doing nothing when it is a real option. For each: cost, risk, time, and what it enables or blocks. Show the decisive difference.
4. **Recommendation** in one line, with the single reason that carries it.
5. **Cost of delay**: what happens if no decision is made by the date.
6. **Next step** once decided: who does what.

One page. Business terms first: money, risk, customers, time. Numbers over adjectives. Technical detail goes in an appendix or a link. Keep facts and recommendation visibly apart, so the reader can disagree with the judgement without doubting the facts. Present each option at its strongest, then recommend.

## Hamburger comprehensibility concept

The Hamburger Verständlichkeitskonzept (Langer, Schulz von Thun, Tausch) names four dimensions. Use them as revision lenses, not a scorecard. Make the explanation easier rather than asking more of the reader.

- **Simplicity (Einfachheit):** familiar words, straightforward sentences, explained domain terms.
- **Structure (Gliederung/Ordnung):** a visible thread, related points together, essentials before detail.
- **Brevity (Kürze/Prägnanz):** cut repetition and digressions; keep necessary context.
- **Stimulating additions (Anregende Zusätze):** a useful example, analogy, or illustration, used sparingly.

Favor simplicity and structure. Balance brevity against completeness.

## STE-100 style

For procedures and technical handovers, borrow from Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100): one intended meaning per term, short sentences, active voice, one instruction per sentence, conditions before actions, the actor or object named explicitly. Keep precise domain terms instead of varying synonyms. Full compliance needs the STE writing rules and controlled dictionary; this is a lightweight approach and does not claim compliance.

## Rules

Value the reader's attention. One idea per section. Short words where a short word exists. Repeat the term instead of pointing back at it. Keep technical detail apart from decisions and concepts. Say nothing about the format beyond the line that names it.

## Deliver

The idea in the chosen format. Apply write-human to the prose.
